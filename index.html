Connected Australia

Exploring internet usage and the digital divide across Australia.

Internet access has become an essential part of everyday life in Australia, supporting communication, education, employment and access to online services. However, being connected to the internet does not necessarily mean that everyone can participate equally in the digital world. Differences in internet access, affordability and digital skills continue to create barriers for some Australians.

This visualisation explores Australia's digital divide by examining changes in internet usage, geographic differences in connectivity and digital inclusion across age, income, employment and education groups. It also investigates how Australians use the internet, including their online activities and preferred devices. Together, these visualisations highlight the progress Australia has made towards digital connectivity and the inequalities that remain.

01 - How Has Internet Use Changed in Australia?

Internet use has become widespread but being online does not necessarily mean being digitally included.

Key finding: Widespread internet use

Reported internet usage increased sharply between 2019 and 2020, before remaining close to 99% in the following years.

However, widespread internet use does not necessarily mean that everyone has reliable access, affordable connections or the digital skills needed to participate online.

This suggests that Australia's digital divide is not only about internet usage, but also about the quality of access and the ability to use digital services effectively.

02 - Where Is Australia Most Connected?

Internet access differs across Australian states and territories, revealing geographic inequalities in digital connectivity.

Key finding: Geographic differences in internet access

In 2025, the Australian Capital Territory recorded the highest Internet Access Index (80.9), while the Northern Territory had the lowest (71.7) representing a difference of 9.2 index points.

The Internet Access Index measures aspects of connectivity, including the types of internet technlogies and data allowances available to users. It does not simply represent the percentage of people with internet access.

These differences suggest that high national internet usage does not necessarily translate into equal access conditions across Australia.

03 - Where Are Digital Skills Strongest?

Digital ability varies across Australian states and territories, highlighting differences in people's ability to use digital technologies.

Key finding: Unequal digital ability across Australia

In 2020, the Australian Capital Territory recorded the highest Digital Ability Index (55.7), while the Northen Territory had the lowest (46.5), a difference of 9.2 index points. This highlights geographic differences in digital ability across Australia.

How to read this map:

Larger circles represent higher Digital Ability Index scores. The index measures digital skills and capabilities rather than the number of people in each state or territory.

04 - Where is NBN fixed-line coverage concentrated across Australia?

This dot map illustrates the geographical distribution of NBN fixed-line coverage across Australia in March 2024, highlighting where fixed-line network infrastructure is concentrated.

Key finding: Uneven fixed-line coverage

NBN fixed-line coverage is geographically concentrated rather than evenly distributed across Australia. The dots highlight areas represented by the fixed-line coverage polygons, while large parts of inland Australia have relatively few dots.

How to read this dot density map:

Each dot represents approximately 25 km² of mapped NBN fixed-line coverage. More dots indicate a greater amount of covered land. Dot positions are sampled and do not represent individual households or exact connection locations.

05 - Which age groups face the greatest digital exclusion?

This heatmap compares digital inclusion levels across six Australian age groups in 2025, revealing differences in digital participation by age.

Key finding:

Digital exclusion increases with age. Among Australians aged 75 and over, 41.4% were highly excluded, compared with just 1.8% of those aged 18 – 34.

How to read this heatmap:

Darker blue cells represent a higher percentage of people within each age group belonging to that digital inclusion category.

06 - How many Australians are digitally excluded?

This waffle chart shows the proportion of Australians experiencing different levels of digital inclusion in 2025.

Key finding:

Around one in five Australians (20.6%) were digitally excluded or highly excluded in 2025, including 9.2% who were highly excluded.

How to read this waffle chart:

Each square represents approximately 1% of Australians. Colours indicate different digital inclusion categories.

07 - How do internet access and digital ability differ by income?

This chart compares Internet Access and Digital Ability Index scores across five income quintiles in 2020, highlighting the differences between internet connectivity and digital skills at different income levels.

Q1 represents the highest income quintile while Q5 represents the lowest income quintile. Both indices are measured on a scale from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater digital inclusion.

Key finding:

Both Internet Access and Digital Ability scores decrease from Q1 to Q5, highlighting differences in digital inclusion across income groups.

Internet Access scores are higher than Digital Ability scores across all five quintiles, with gaps ranging from 33.3 to 38.5 index points. In the lowest income quintile (Q5), Internet Access scores 73.5 compared with only 36.3 for Digital Ability, suggesting that access to the internet alone does not guarantee strong digital skills.

How to read this chart:

Each pair of dots represents Internet Access and Digital Ability scores for one income quintile. Longer connecting lines indicate a larger difference between the two indices.

08 - How does digital inclusion differ across demographic groups?

This chart compares Internet Access, Affordability and Digital Ability Index scores across income, employment and education groups in Australia in 2020.

Use the dropdown menu to switch between demographic categories. Each line represents one group, allowing differences across the three dimensions to be compared.

Key finding:

Income: The highest income group (Q1) scores higher than the lowest income group (Q5) across all three dimensions. Affordability shows the largest difference, with a 45.8 point gap, highlighting the financial barriers faced by lower income households.

Employment: Employed people generally achieve higher scores than unemployed people and those not in the labour force (NILF). Internet Access is highest among employed people (81.3), while Digital Ability is lowest among NILF (40.8).

Education: People with tertiary education achieve the highest scores across all three dimensions. Those with lower educational attainment have the lowest Digital Ability score (34.8), suggesting that education is closely associated with digital skills.

Overall, the differences are particularly noticeable in Affordability and Digital Ability, showing that digital inclusion involves more than simply having internet access.

09 - How have Australians' online activity preferences changed over time?

This chart shows how the rankings of six selected online activities changed in Australia between 2020 and 2025. Rankings are based on the percentage of respondents who performed each activity in the past six months.

Key finding:

News and information consistently ranked first, followed by online shopping and video calls. These three activities maintained their positions throughout 2020 – 2025, showing that their relative popularity remained stable.

Telehealth and working from home exchanged fourth and fifth positions several times during this period. Their changing rankings suggest that the relative popularity of these two activities varied over time.

Online studying consistently ranked sixth among the selected activities, indicating that it was the least common of the six activities throughout the period.

How to read this chart:

Rank 1 represents the most common activity, while Rank 6 represents the least common. Each coloured line shows an activity's ranking from 2020 to 2025. Hover over the points to view the percentage of respondents and ranking for each year.

10 - How do online activity preferences differ across generations?

This radar chart compares participation in six online activities across three Australian age groups in 2025, highlighting differences in how younger, middle-aged and older Australians use the internet.

Key finding:

Online shopping and accessing news and information were common across all three age groups. However, Australians aged 18 – 24 had considerably higher participation in online studying and video calling than those aged 75 and over.

Among Australians aged 18 – 24, 66.7% participated in online studying, compared with only 7.8% of those aged 75 and over, a difference of 58.9 percentage points.

How to read this chart:

Each coloured line represents an age group. The six axes represent different online activities and points farther from the centre indicate higher participation percentages.

11 - How have Australians' internet access devices changed over time?

This chart compares the devices Australians used to access the internet in 2017 and 2025, highlighting changes in how people connect to online services.

Key finding: Changing internet access habits

Mobile phones remained the most commonly used device, increasing from 75.2% in 2017 to 97.1% in 2025, a rise of 21.9 percentage points.

Smart TVs experienced the largest increase among the five selected devices, rising from 31.2% to 64.3%, a difference of 33.1 percentage points.

In contrast, desktop computer usage decreased from 48.4% to 40.7%. These changes suggest that Australians are increasingly using mobile and connected entertainment devices to access the internet.
